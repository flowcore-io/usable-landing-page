The communication problem

Every outward message borrows the organisation’s authority

A website page, support reply, customer update, article, social post, video description, podcast summary, sales deck, or press response may be written by different people in different systems. The reader does not see those boundaries. They experience one organisation and assume its claims agree.

That makes outward communication an operational flow, not only a writing task. A polished draft can still contain an outdated product fact, a customer detail that was never approved for publication, an incident hypothesis presented as a cause, a promised date that delivery has not accepted, or a claim copied from an old presentation after its evidence changed.

Usable can hold the governed memory beneath this work: the research, product truth, approved claims, audience decisions, source permissions, canonical assets, review outcomes, channel adaptations, corrections, and verified audience learning. People and AI tools can retrieve that context when drafting. The organisation still decides what is true, publishable, appropriate for the audience, and ready to leave the private boundary.

The principle: create from shared evidence, publish through explicit authority, then return verified learning to the memory that will shape the next message.

This goes beyond a single answering surface

An assistant that answers from approved documentation is one useful surface. The same governed memory can support the full outward-content system:

  • website and product pages that state the current public truth;
  • articles, guides, case studies, newsletters, and search-oriented reference content;
  • professional-network posts, community updates, short-form posts, and discussion responses;
  • video scripts, descriptions, captions, podcast summaries, show notes, and repurposed clips;
  • presentations, sales material, media briefs, press responses, and event follow-up;
  • customer announcements, delivery updates, release explanations, support replies, and known-issue communication; and
  • embedded or internal assistants that draft from the same approved source set.

The output formats differ, but the hard questions repeat: Who is the audience? What are we allowed to say? Which source is canonical? What evidence supports each claim? Who owns approval? Where will the item be published? What happens when the source changes or a reader corrects us?

What Usable provides—and what you configure around it

Usable currently provides workspaces with roles and visibility boundaries, focused memory fragments, built-in and custom fragment types, collections, file attachments, searchable frontmatter and metadata, semantic and agentic search, full-fragment retrieval, and update operations. Teams and approved tools can access this memory through the web interface, Usable Chat, MCP, or the API. Credentials can be limited to the workspace permissions an integration requires.

Teams may model editorial states—draft, in review, approved, published, corrected, superseded, and archived—in templates, searchable frontmatter, fragment content, and connected workflows. Those states describe the editorial process; they do not make Usable the system of record for scheduling, delivery, publication, or live status. The destination system still owns whether an item is actually queued, sent, rendered, visible, corrected, or withdrawn.

Usable is not a universal publishing scheduler, social account manager, legal approver, email sender, video editor, press office, or replacement for the organisation’s content-management and customer systems. Those are workflow and integration choices. Usable stores and retrieves the governed context that those systems and their operators use.

An illustrative automation might create a draft fragment when a support theme crosses a threshold, notify a reviewer, and copy an approved adaptation into a publishing system. That flow is possible to build; it is not safe to describe as automatic product behaviour until the organisation has configured and verified it.

Model the content system before generating more content

A useful workspace separates durable truth from temporary production state. Collections can group material by audience, product area, communication programme, or channel without placing every item in one large document. Custom fragment types can match the organisation’s own editorial process.

A practical set of connected records

  • Product fact: the verified capability, limitation, availability state, responsible owner, evidence, effective date, and approved public wording.
  • Research note: primary sources, observed change, facts, inferences, counterevidence, implications, review date, and confidence.
  • Audience insight: the problem language, context, question, or objection; where it was observed; whether it is representative; and what remains unknown.
  • Content brief: primary reader, situation, desired outcome, central claim, evidence, limitations, format, source owner, permission status, reviewer, channel, and deadline.
  • Canonical asset: the approved complete explanation or evidence pack from which narrower adaptations may be made.
  • Channel adaptation: the derivative copy, its audience and constraints, the canonical asset it came from, status, reviewer, destination, and published URL.
  • Review record: claims checked, sources inspected, permissions confirmed, corrections required, residual risks, approver, and decision.
  • Correction or retirement: what changed, why, affected outputs, replacement source, owner, correction date, and follow-up.

Customer records, contracts, incident investigations, unreleased product plans, and employee information should remain in their controlled workspaces. The content workspace can link to those sources or receive a sanitised, approved fact fragment. Connection does not require duplication or wider access.

The end-to-end outward-content workflow

  1. Capture the trigger. Record the customer question, product change, verified incident learning, sales objection, research signal, event, operational observation, or editorial need that justifies communication.
  2. Define audience and intent. Name one primary reader, their situation, what they should understand or do, and what the organisation should not imply. “Everyone” is not a useful audience.
  3. Research before drafting. Search the relevant workspaces, prefer primary and current sources, retrieve full fragments, collect counterevidence, and distinguish fact from inference and hypothesis.
  4. Choose the canonical source. Decide where the complete approved explanation will live. Other channels should adapt it, not become competing sources of truth.
  5. Build the brief and claim ledger. List each material claim, its evidence, permission, owner, freshness, limitation, and required reviewer. Mark unsupported claims before prose makes them persuasive.
  6. Draft for the canonical format. Write the complete explanation with sources, context, limitations, useful examples, and a practical next step. Generated text remains a draft.
  7. Adapt by channel. Preserve the factual core while changing length, pacing, voice, visual context, call to action, and metadata for each audience and surface.
  8. Run review gates. Check product truth, customer permission, confidentiality, privacy, contract scope, legal or regulatory implications, brand voice, accessibility, and technical accuracy as applicable.
  9. Approve explicitly. Record who approved which version for which destination. Approval of an article does not automatically approve every excerpt, screenshot, or customer email derived from it.
  10. Publish through the right system. Deliver the approved version via the CMS, customer channel, media process, event system, or distribution tool that owns that surface. Verify the rendered or delivered result.
  11. Distribute without duplicating truth. Link or adapt from the canonical asset, record reuse, and keep the channel item connected to its source and permission state.
  12. Listen and classify feedback. Separate questions, disagreement, corrections, demand signals, support issues, and vanity reactions. Route operational findings to their real owners.
  13. Correct, maintain, and archive. Update the source, trace affected derivatives, publish a visible correction where material, and retire outputs whose evidence or purpose has expired.

Start with a brief that can survive handoffs

Good content intake prevents late-stage disagreement. A request such as “write a post about the new feature” hides nearly every decision that matters. The brief should contain enough context for a researcher, subject-matter expert, writer, reviewer, designer, and publisher to work without repeatedly reconstructing the purpose.

Brief fieldQuestion it answers
Primary audience and situationWho needs this now, and what is happening when they encounter it?
Intent and practical outcomeWhat should the reader understand, decide, or do?

Evidence and source ownerWhat proves the claim, and who can confirm its meaning?

Research produces a claim ledger, not a pile of links

Search the latest relevant market, product, customer, engineering, delivery, and incident fragments before drafting. Use primary sources first: approved documentation, released product behaviour, deployment or test evidence, signed decisions, customer-approved material, and attributable public sources. A presentation or old post may reveal a useful angle, but it is not automatically current product truth.

For each material statement, classify the result:

  • Fact: directly supported by a current source.
  • Inference: an interpretation that should be labelled or confirmed.
  • Hypothesis: something worth testing, not a public result.
  • Action: a next step supported by the evidence.
  • Unknown: uncorroborated, conflicting, inaccessible, or stale.

One internal fact-check demonstrated the importance of this discipline: a plausible case-study narrative combined chronology, architecture, automation, deployment, cost, and customer-proof statements more confidently than the available records justified. The useful outcome was not to discard the story. It was to narrow wording, identify substantiation needed, separate interview recollection from verified record, and leave residual uncertainty visible.

Publication rule: uncorroborated does not always mean false. It means the claim needs evidence, attribution, narrower wording, or removal before publication.

One canonical source, many purposeful adaptations

Choose an owned, stable location for the complete explanation: a product page for official capability, documentation for instructions, a maintained customer update for an agreed change, an article for a developed argument, or an approved press asset for company facts. The canonical source should have an owner, stable address, status, effective date, supporting sources, and correction path.

Adaptations should stand on their own but remain traceable. A professional-network post may focus on one consequence. A short video description should state the insight without narrating what viewers can see. A podcast summary may need conversational context and listening cues. A newsletter may connect several canonical items. A support reply may use only the specific approved fact and the customer’s next step. A press response may require attributable, tightly verified wording.

“Create once, paste everywhere” is not adaptation. It produces channel-inappropriate copy and uncontrolled duplication. The better model is: establish the factual core once, then deliberately reshape it for the audience while preserving meaning, permissions, limitations, and a route back to the source.

Use risk-based publication gates

Not every item needs the same process. A simple sourced explanation of an already-public fact should not wait behind the same gate as a customer case study, security claim, incident update, or contractual change. Define the tiers before production pressure arrives.

GateTypical materialMinimum control
RoutineAlready-public facts, approved evergreen guidance, sourced interpretation, or low-risk educational materialAuthor checks the current source, audience, voice, metadata, and destination
Source-owner reviewCustomer stories, screenshots, quotes, roadmap references, performance, security, privacy, residency, benchmark, partner, pricing, or service claimsNamed subject owner verifies facts and permission; content owner records approval and residual limits
Specialist approvalPersonal data, regulated subjects, contractual interpretation, live incidents, financial commitments, legal positions, sensitive comparisons, or material correctionsAuthorised specialist approves exact wording, audience, timing, and delivery path
Do not publishConfidential records, unsupported comparisons, unannounced plans, private incident evidence, secrets, personal cases, or claims with unresolved material contradictionsKeep private, gather evidence, sanitise, or abandon the item

AI review can help find gaps, ambiguity, weak structure, and inconsistent voice. A second model or reviewer can challenge the first draft. Neither becomes the approver simply because it returns a score or fluent revision. Human authority and source ownership remain explicit.

Move from private evidence to public explanation deliberately

The most valuable stories often begin in restricted work: a customer engagement, contract decision, support case, technical investigation, deployment, incident, internal correction, or commercial discussion. Publishing the raw record would expose context the audience neither needs nor has permission to see.

  1. Identify the transferable lesson. Separate the organisational pattern from names, systems, dates, identities, and confidential detail.
  2. List every factual dependency. Mark which facts are public, permissioned, sanitised, attributable, or prohibited.
  3. Ask the source owner. Confirm that the lesson, quote, screenshot, outcome, and implied endorsement may be used in the intended channel.
  4. Create an approved public fact. Store the narrow wording and its limits in the outward-content workspace rather than granting broad access to the private source.
  5. Review re-identification risk. Removing a name is insufficient when role, timing, geography, architecture, or unusual outcome still identifies the subject.
  6. Keep the private record private. The published item links internally to its provenance; the audience sees only what was approved.

Approval is not publication

A content record marked published should correspond to a real, verified output. The publishing system owns rendering, scheduling, delivery, account access, and live status. Usable can preserve the approved source, destination, status, URL, and evidence of verification.

A demonstrated content workflow exposed what happens when this distinction breaks: an item marked as a draft was visible publicly. The response required finding the feedback, reproducing the issue, taking down or correcting the output, updating the underlying content state, deploying through the site workflow, verifying the live result, drafting a response, and recording an instruction to prevent recurrence.

Minimum post-publish verification

  • The intended version is live or delivered to the correct audience.
  • Draft, embargoed, private, or superseded material is not exposed.
  • Title, description, canonical address, preview image, captions, links, and structured metadata are correct.
  • Claims, dates, screenshots, transcripts, and calls to action match the approved version.
  • The published URL or delivery record is linked back to its canonical asset and review record.
  • A named owner knows how to correct or withdraw the item.

Customer communication needs one coordinated fact set

A change that affects customers may require an owned explanation, direct customer message, in-product notice, support talking points, FAQ, social update, sales context, and follow-up. Writing each independently invites contradictions. Build the canonical change record first:

  • what is changing and what is not;
  • who is affected and when;
  • the verified reason that may be stated publicly;
  • customer impact, choices, migration or workaround;
  • contractual or individual exceptions;
  • support ownership and escalation triggers;
  • approved wording, prohibited claims, and unanswered questions; and
  • how response patterns and outcomes will be reviewed.

Support staff should acknowledge concern rather than defend the copy. Questions requiring account state, money movement, legal interpretation, confidential commitments, or destructive action must move to the authorised process. Recurring confusion is evidence that the canonical explanation, product experience, agreement, or support procedure needs work—not merely that another post is needed.

The compounding loop is controlled: engineering proves a limitation; support explains it accurately; marketing turns the lesson into a useful guide; sales reuses the approved asset; customer questions expose missing clarity; product and documentation improve; onboarding teaches the corrected explanation; and the next outward item retrieves that stronger memory.

Each function retains its boundary. Marketing does not edit the incident cause. Support does not reinterpret a contract. Engineering does not approve a customer quote. A public assistant retrieves the approved outward fact, not the private evidence behind it.

Feedback is input, not automatic truth

Comments, replies, support contacts, event questions, search queries, watch behaviour, sales conversations, and press follow-up can reveal language and demand the organisation did not anticipate. Store the signal with its source, date, audience, context, and confidence. Do not promote a single reaction into a market conclusion.

Classify feedback before acting:

  • Correction: a factual error or exposed draft that requires immediate verification.
  • Question: information the audience could not find or understand.
  • Objection: a trust, relevance, timing, risk, or value concern.
  • Operational issue: a support, product, delivery, billing, or incident signal that belongs with another owner.
  • Content signal: a topic, example, format, or channel worth testing.
  • Noise: activity that does not support a decision.

Verified learning should update a maintained insight or canonical asset. Do not create isolated “lesson” fragments for every reaction when a current synthesis can be improved.

Corrections, versions, and retirement are part of publishing

When a source changes, identify every derivative that relied on it. The canonical asset should state the new fact, effective date, reason for change, source, owner, and which prior version it supersedes. Channel records should link to the replacement or be marked for review, correction, or retirement.

For a material public error, correct the live item visibly, date the correction, explain the changed point without reproducing restricted evidence, and notify directly affected audiences through the appropriate channel. For a minor formatting fix, a silent update may be reasonable under the organisation’s policy. The decision should be consistent, not improvised based on embarrassment.

Archive when the audience need has ended, the format no longer works, permission has expired, a campaign or event is over, the source is superseded, or the content cannot be maintained. Archived material can remain useful internal evidence while being excluded from current retrieval and distribution.

Ownership makes memory trustworthy

Use private workspaces for research, customer context, contracts, incidents, unpublished plans, and drafts. Use carefully curated public or customer-safe collections for approved facts and guidance. Owners control the domain; collaborators maintain content; viewers retrieve without changing it. An answering or drafting integration normally needs read access, not the ability to publish or alter the source.

Every reusable item should carry enough governance to answer:

  • Who owns the truth, the content, the approval, and the destination?
  • Is the item research, draft, in review, approved, published, corrected, superseded, or archived?
  • Which audience and channels are permitted?
  • Which evidence supports it, and when was that evidence checked?
  • What may not be claimed, named, quoted, or shown?
  • When must it be reviewed, and what event should trigger earlier review?

Stored material is not authoritative merely because an AI can retrieve it. Authority comes from provenance, ownership, scope, current status, evidence, and review.

Reach and reactions can support interpretation, but they are not evidence that a claim is true or a workflow is useful. Choose measures that match the audience intent: understanding, qualified conversation, task completion, reduced confusion, safe activation, or better decisions.

A practical rollout

  • Weeks 1–2 — map truth and authority: choose one recurring outward flow, identify the private sources, canonical public source, owners, reviewers, destinations, permissions, and known failure modes.
  • Weeks 3–4 — create the content model: add product facts, research notes, briefs, canonical assets, adaptations, reviews, feedback, and correction records. Set roles and statuses.
  • Weeks 5–6 — run one complete item: research, draft, fact-check, approve, publish, verify, adapt, distribute, collect feedback, and make one maintenance decision.
  • Weeks 7–8 — connect one intake and one destination: use the API, MCP, or webhook pattern where helpful, but keep approval and publishing explicit until the integration is proven.
  • Weeks 9–12 — add cross-functional loops: bring in approved customer, support, engineering, incident, and contract learning without widening private access.

Limits to keep visible

  • Usable cannot prove an absent fact. If the source is missing or conflicting, the workflow must investigate or narrow the claim.
  • Generation does not create permission. Rewriting private information does not make it safe to publish.
  • Retrieval does not replace review. A current source can still be misapplied to the wrong audience or channel.
  • Adaptation can change meaning. Shortening copy may remove the limitation that made the original accurate.
  • Integrations can publish the wrong state. Draft, approved, scheduled, live, corrected, and archived must remain distinct and verifiable.
  • Feedback is biased and incomplete. Visible reactions should inform tests, not become automatic strategy.
  • Maintenance is permanent. More content without owners, review triggers, and retirement creates a larger misinformation surface.

The outcome: Usable helps an organisation remember what it knows, why it believes it, who may use it, where it was published, and what changed—so outward communication becomes more consistent, correctable, reusable, and useful over time.
