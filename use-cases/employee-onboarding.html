The onboarding problem

Onboarding is a controlled transfer of decision rights

An account checklist can get someone logged in. It cannot tell them which promise to a customer takes priority, why a public claim is worded carefully, which engineering standard must not be bypassed, or when an operational change needs explicit approval. That context is usually split across contracts, conversations, plans, code, runbooks, incident records, and experienced colleagues.

The result is a false choice: either give the new person broad access and hope they find the right material, or keep them dependent on a few people who repeatedly reconstruct the context. A safe onboarding system does neither. It defines what the role must be able to accomplish, grants only the context required for those outcomes, and pairs retrieval with staged practice and human review.

Usable gives that working context an organisation-owned home. Workspaces separate collaboration domains; fragments preserve focused knowledge; collections curate learning paths; files retain source evidence; and the dashboard, REST API, and MCP server make the same governed material available to people, scripts, and approved AI clients. The aim is not to replace welcome, mentorship, or judgement. It is to make those conversations start from shared evidence.

Begin with role outcomes, not a reading list

Define the decisions and results the person should eventually own. A role outcome is observable: prepare a customer handover that reflects the signed scope; draft public content that can be traced to approved evidence; implement a change using the organisation’s engineering standards; or investigate an incident without changing production before approval.

For every outcome, document five things:

  • Boundary: what the role owns, contributes to, observes, or must escalate.
  • Evidence: which fragments, collections, source files, systems, and people are authoritative.
  • Standard: the definition of done, required checks, and known exceptions.
  • Decision right: what the person can decide independently and what requires review.
  • Feedback route: where questions, contradictions, and verified learning should be returned.

This makes onboarding useful for a new employee, an internal transfer, a contractor, a returning colleague, or temporary cover. The material follows the responsibility rather than assuming that everyone needs the same tour.

Give each role a governed view of the whole organisation

Good work crosses departmental boundaries, but access should not. A new colleague may need the approved conclusion from another area without seeing that area’s entire workspace. In Usable, the workspace is the collaboration boundary. A role path can point to collections in several permitted workspaces, and selected fragments can be linked across workspaces where membership and permissions allow. That creates connected context without creating one unrestricted knowledge pool.

The connection matters. Customer commitments can become delivery acceptance criteria. Engineering constraints can prevent marketing from promising something the product does not support. Incident learning can update runbooks, customer communication, and future design standards. Questions from a new colleague can expose a stale responsibility or an unsupported public claim. Each area improves the others through approved conclusions—not unrestricted visibility.

Compounding does not mean universal access. It means that a verified lesson can be promoted by its owner into the appropriate audience, linked to its source, and retrieved in later work. The confidential evidence may remain private while an approved, sanitised conclusion becomes reusable.

Grant access before assigning content

Before building a learning path, map the role to workspace access and write authority. Usable documents four workspace roles or access states: Owner, Collaborator, Viewer, and Subscriber. Access can come from direct workspace membership, a subscription to a public workspace, or an eligible organisation role on an organisation-owned workspace. These paths are not interchangeable: a direct member has an assigned workspace role, a subscriber has read-style access to public material, and an organisation owner or administrator may receive organisation-derived access without a direct workspace-membership record.

  1. Inventory the outcomes. List the decisions, systems, customer contexts, and escalation duties the role actually needs.
  2. Choose the workspace boundary. Add the person only to workspaces required for those outcomes. Use Viewer access for reference material; use Collaborator access only where maintaining knowledge is part of the role; reserve Owner for full workspace management.
  3. Scope each AI client separately. Use OAuth where supported or a dedicated token per client or automation. First verify that every required workspace appears in that credential’s workspace-and-permission surface. Dashboard visibility alone does not prove that a personal access token or MCP connection can be scoped to the same workspace. Organisation-derived workspaces may currently be absent from token scope selection. If a required workspace is missing, use an explicitly governed direct membership or integration design approved by the workspace owner; do not assume or broaden access implicitly.
  4. Test the negative path. Confirm the person and their AI client can retrieve the intended source and cannot retrieve a restricted example. A successful search proves reachability, not correct isolation. Repeat this test after changing membership, credential scope, or organisation role.
  5. Keep secrets elsewhere. Passwords, token values, private keys, raw personal records, and sensitive exports do not belong in onboarding fragments.

Access may grow after supervised work demonstrates that the responsibility has changed. It should not grow merely because the person has been present for a certain number of days. Record which access path supports the human workflow and which separately supports any API or AI client.

Build a path through evidence, practice, and responsibility

A collection is a curated group of fragments inside a workspace. Use it to create a purposeful route, not to mirror every document the team owns. When a role spans workspaces, use a short role index that points to the permitted collection in each area.

A practical role path

  1. Orientation. Team purpose, role outcomes, vocabulary, collaboration boundaries, named ownership functions, and escalation routes.
  2. Sources and standards. Current policies, approved commitments, decision records, recipes, templates, definitions of done, and representative accepted work.
  3. Guided retrieval. Give the person real questions. Search identifies candidate fragments; the full fragment and attached evidence are read before a decision is made.
  4. Safe rehearsal. Use a past, synthetic, or non-production case. Ask the person to show which sources they used, what they inferred, and where confidence ends.
  5. Supervised live work. Complete a real task with a reviewer and explicit approval gates. The reviewer focuses on judgement and exceptions rather than repeating the whole procedure.
  6. Verified contribution. Turn discovered gaps into drafts or proposed updates. The knowledge owner checks the evidence before publishing, linking, or replacing an authoritative fragment.

Attach a long policy, agreement, diagram, or report to the fragment that explains why and when it matters. The focused fragment improves retrieval; the attached source preserves evidence. Titles, summaries, fragment types, tags, status, ownership, and review dates make the material usable outside the original collection.

Practise the real flow at a safe level

Our own workflows illustrate why staged practice matters. Consultancy knowledge can be structured as customer, project, interaction, formal-document, recipe, and solution records. Marketing knowledge can separate research, plans, content ideas, published content events, posts, transcripts, and style guidance. Engineering knowledge can distinguish standards, architectural decisions, pathway contracts, PRDs, recipes, templates, violations, and verified solutions. Operations can preserve an evidence-led investigation separately from the quick solution that should be found next time.

The specific structure will differ by organisation, but the training pattern is reusable:

  • Customer-facing role: reconstruct the approved scope and next action from a sanitised relationship history; do not infer a commitment from an informal note.
  • Content role: turn one approved product fact into a draft for two channels, apply the correct voice, identify every claim that still needs evidence, and keep the result in draft until approval.
  • Developer role: search standards and prior solutions before planning, follow the repository’s actual architecture, run validation, and document a verified exception or reusable fix after review.
  • Operations role: reproduce the symptom, assess health, inspect traces, logs, events, dependencies, metrics, and history, form an evidence-backed hypothesis, request approval before remediation, then verify the original failure path.

This is more than reading comprehension. It tests whether the person can find the right source, respect a boundary, recognise uncertainty, and produce evidence that another person can review.

One first assignment can connect the organisation

Imagine a new delivery lead preparing a customer update after a reliability issue affected a planned milestone. They are permitted to read the delivery workspace, approved product guidance, a sanitised incident conclusion, and the relevant public-message guide. They cannot read unrelated customers, raw production evidence, restricted commercial notes, or private content drafts.

  1. Start from the commitment. Retrieve the approved scope, acceptance criteria, decision history, and latest handover—not an old email remembered by a colleague.
  2. Check product and engineering truth. Read the current standard and implementation status before describing what can be delivered.
  3. Use the verified operational conclusion. The outage report explains impact and prevention; the new colleague does not improvise from raw logs or repeat an unconfirmed hypothesis.
  4. Apply message governance. Draft the update in the appropriate voice, distinguish fact from expectation, and flag claims requiring owner approval.
  5. Review the decision. Delivery, technical, and communication owners approve the parts they govern. The customer receives one coherent update.
  6. Return the learning. If the scope fragment was ambiguous, the incident conclusion lacked a customer-safe summary, or the style guide omitted this situation, each gap is proposed to the relevant owner.

The first task therefore improves more than the new person’s understanding. It tests the links between commitments, implementation, operations, and communication. Once owners verify the corrections, a later handover, campaign, feature plan, or incident response starts with better context.

Treat questions as governed maintenance signals

New colleagues are good at finding hidden assumptions because they cannot silently fill gaps from years of experience. Make that useful without letting uncertainty become policy.

Do not measure contribution by fragment volume. A small, owned, current path is better than a large archive of plausible answers. Draft, published, and archived states can help communicate maturity, but the organisation still needs a clear rule for who can declare a source authoritative.

Make ownership and review visible

Assign ownership by knowledge area, not by whoever happened to write the page. The owner does not need to answer every question; they are accountable for resolving authority and approving changes.

Where automation is useful, webhooks, REST integrations, MCP clients, or cross-workspace linking rules can move an approved workflow forward. Preview broad link rules, select only required event types, and keep a human approval gate where the action changes authority, access, public content, customer commitments, or production systems.

Role change and offboarding are part of onboarding design

A durable role path should make access removable and responsibility transferable. When a person changes role or leaves, reverse the onboarding map instead of relying on memory.

  • Review workspace memberships against the person’s new or departing responsibilities.
  • Revoke dedicated personal access tokens and AI-client connections that are no longer required; rotate any credential that may have been exposed.
  • Transfer ownership for current fragments, collections, decisions, reviews, customer actions, and operational follow-ups.
  • Resolve personal drafts: promote verified material, hand it to an owner, or archive it. Do not turn unfinished notes into authoritative knowledge by default.
  • Capture a structured handover around outcomes, open decisions, evidence, risks, and next review—not a narrative dump of everything the person knows.
  • Test that required successors can retrieve the material and that the former role can no longer reach restricted examples.

Usable provides workspace membership, roles, token scoping and revocation, content status, collections, and integration surfaces. Automatic HR-system provisioning, identity-provider group changes, scheduled access certification, and application-account removal are workflow or integration patterns that must be configured and verified in the organisation’s other systems.

Set a baseline before promising improvement. Retrieval counts, AI-generated summaries, or large fragment totals are not proof that someone can perform safely. Pair quantitative measures with sampled task review and feedback from the new colleague, their reviewer, and the knowledge owners.

What Usable provides—and what your workflow must provide

Current Usable capabilities

Usable currently provides workspace-scoped collaboration with Owner, Collaborator, Viewer, and Subscriber access; memory fragments with metadata and status; custom fragment types; collections; files and attachments; semantic and agentic discovery; full-fragment retrieval; cross-workspace fragment links where permitted; webhooks; dashboard access; REST; and MCP. Human workspace access may come from direct membership, public subscription, or an eligible organisation role.

Those access paths are not represented consistently on every surface. In particular, an organisation-derived workspace can be visible in the dashboard while remaining absent from the personal-access-token workspace selector used to scope API or MCP credentials. An approved AI client can search or fetch a workspace only after that workspace is available to the credential and the credential has the required read permissions. Create and update permissions must be granted separately. Treat dashboard visibility, token scope, and successful MCP access as three checks—not one assumption.

Illustrative workflow and integration patterns

Role matrices, first-week schedules, practice scenarios, approval chains, HR or identity-provider automation, publication pipelines, CI/CD gates, observability queries, production remediation, and scheduled access reviews are not created merely by storing knowledge in Usable. They require organisational design, configured integrations, appropriate external systems, and accountable people. Cross-workspace links also require deliberate permission and content-safety review; they do not bypass workspace access.

An AI assistant can retrieve the context its verified credential is allowed to use and help draft a plan, update, or proposed fragment. It cannot determine legal authority, validate an unverified customer commitment, approve public publication, grant itself production access, or remediate an incident without the organisation’s controls. Usable makes governed context available at the point of work; the organisation remains responsible for the decision.

The outcome: every safe role transition becomes a test of the organisation’s memory. Each verified correction improves later onboarding, delivery, marketing, engineering, and operations—compounding through connected knowledge while ownership and access remain explicit.
