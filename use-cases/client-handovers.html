The relationship problem

A handover fails long before the handover meeting

A client relationship begins with discovery, promises, assumptions, and constraints. It continues through an agreement, meetings, delivery choices, acceptance, support, incidents, and commercial reviews. Yet organisations often document only the final deliverable. The signed agreement sits in one system, meeting decisions in another, work in a project tracker, evidence in repositories, and operational learning in the heads of the people who responded.

The result is not merely a documentation gap. Sales cannot tell whether a public promise is safe to repeat. Delivery cannot see why a requirement was framed a particular way. Support cannot connect a recurring question to the original scope. A new relationship owner cannot distinguish a binding obligation from an idea discussed in a meeting. The client must keep re-explaining its own history.

Usable can provide the governed memory that connects those records. It preserves the explanation, provenance, ownership, and relationship between facts while volatile or legally authoritative facts remain in their source systems. The goal is a continuous client memory, not a larger folder of files.

Keep each system authoritative for what it does

Usable is not inherently the place where a contract is signed, an invoice is posted, a ticket is closed, or a deployment is approved. Treat those tools as systems of record. In Usable, capture the durable context needed to understand and use their records: what an obligation means, which decision it affected, who owns it, where the source lives, when it was last verified, and what changed because of it.

Truth rule: fetch changing facts such as balances, ticket state, deployment health, and agreement status from their live source. Preserve durable learning and decision context in Usable, with a reference back to that source.

Model the relationship as connected, reviewable records

A practical consultancy model uses focused fragments instead of one ever-growing client document. The exact fragment types can follow the organisation’s language, but the responsibilities should stay distinct:

  • Relationship profile: why the relationship exists, the client’s operating context, stakeholder roles, current themes, communication routes, owner, and next review. Store only necessary personal data.
  • Opportunity or discovery record: the problem being explored, desired outcomes, evidence gathered, constraints, open questions, and the claims made during discovery.
  • Agreement context: a reference to the executed source, effective dates, scope, obligations, responsibilities, acceptance conditions, confidentiality or data-handling constraints, and what is explicitly excluded.
  • Project or service profile: outcomes, milestones, dependencies, delivery roles, health, risks, decisions, evidence, support model, and links to the authoritative delivery tools.
  • Interaction record: date, participants by role, topics, decisions, action owners, due dates, follow-up, and source material. A meeting summary is not an agreement unless the authorised change process says it is.
  • Decision record: the question, options, chosen path, reason, approver, affected obligation or outcome, evidence, consequences, and conditions that should trigger review.
  • Operational record: runbooks, known failure modes, escalation paths, service expectations, incident conclusions, and verified recovery steps.
  • Reusable practice: a sanitised Recipe, Template, Solution, or standard created only after client identifiers, private architecture, commercial terms, and other restricted context have been removed and reviewed.

Use stable titles, types, tags, summaries, status, source references, owners, and review dates. Collections can curate an agreement pack, delivery review, or handover set without turning those fragments into copies. Files and attachments can provide evidence, but the surrounding fragment should explain what the file is, whether it is current, and how it may be used.

The client-memory lifecycle

  1. Establish the relationship boundary. Create the private workspace or approved project boundary, assign an accountable relationship owner, invite only the necessary participants, and define what must never be stored. Record where CRM, contract, project, finance, support, and operational truth lives.
  2. Turn discovery into a traceable brief. Capture the client’s situation, desired outcomes, constraints, assumptions, risks, open questions, and the promises already made. Link important claims to evidence. Separate what the client said, what the team inferred, and what has been formally agreed.
  3. Translate the executed agreement into working context. Reference the signed source and write an obligation map the team can use: deliverable or continuing responsibility, owner on each side, acceptance evidence, dependency, due or review date, and escalation path. Never silently rewrite the contract in memory.
  4. Create a kickoff baseline. Confirm scope, non-goals, ways of working, decision authority, communication cadence, data handling, success signals, environments, support boundaries, and handover expectations. Mark unresolved items as unresolved rather than letting assumptions appear as facts.
  5. Capture interactions and decisions during delivery. After a material meeting or review, preserve the outcome, not a transcript dump. Link decisions to the requirement, obligation, task, source evidence, and affected runbook. Give every action and decision an owner and review condition.
  6. Prove delivery as it happens. For each milestone, connect the intended outcome to acceptance criteria, tests, demonstrations, release records, training, operational readiness, known limitations, and client acknowledgement. Keep actual task and release state in the delivery system.
  7. Control changes explicitly. Record the request, reason, affected scope or obligation, schedule and operating impact, options considered, decision authority, approved source, and follow-up actions. Until the authorised source changes, a discussion or draft is context, not a new commitment.
  8. Build the handover before the end. Curate the accepted scope, decision trail, operating model, runbooks, ownership, support route, risks, open work, access dependencies, and evidence into a handover collection. Review it with the receiving team while the delivery team can still answer gaps.
  9. Continue the memory through service and renewal. Support questions, incidents, improvements, and reviews update the same relationship context. At renewal, compare the live agreement record with delivered evidence, unresolved obligations, changed assumptions, service learning, and proposed next outcomes. The commercial system still records the renewal.
  10. Close deliberately. Confirm final acceptance or termination status in the authoritative system, transfer ownership, settle open actions, apply retention policy, revoke unnecessary membership and integration tokens, and archive superseded guidance. Export or delete only under the agreed contractual, legal, and operational process.

Make scope and change control understandable

Scope disputes often arise because three different statements are treated as equivalent: “we discussed it,” “we intend to do it,” and “we are obligated to do it.” A usable record keeps them separate. An interaction can document the request; a decision can document the assessment; the authorised contract or change-order system records the commitment.

A change record should answer: What prompted the request? Which original assumption, outcome, obligation, dependency, date, or operating cost is affected? What alternatives were considered? Who is authorised to decide? Where is the approved source? What must delivery, support, documentation, training, billing, or marketing update as a consequence?

This trace protects both sides. The client can see how a change reached delivery. The team can see which statements are still proposals. Future maintainers can understand why a seemingly small choice carries a contractual, security, or operational constraint.

A handover is a retrieval test, not a presentation

The receiving team should be able to answer real questions without reconstructing the engagement: What outcome was accepted? What remains out of scope? Who owns the service? Where is the authoritative agreement? How is a common failure diagnosed? Which dependency can stop the process? What is the escalation route? Which risks were consciously accepted, by whom, and under what conditions?

Use a collection to curate the handover, while keeping each record independently maintainable. Include:

  • purpose, accepted outcomes, scope, exclusions, assumptions, and current status;
  • obligation and decision maps with owners, sources, dates, and review conditions;
  • architecture and data-flow explanations appropriate for the receiving audience;
  • release and acceptance evidence, known limitations, deferred work, and residual risk;
  • operating procedures, monitoring expectations, support routes, escalation and recovery steps;
  • access dependencies and references without embedding credentials or private tokens;
  • continuing-service cadence, knowledge owner, review date, and closure or renewal conditions.

Then ask the receiving team to retrieve and explain the answers using the dashboard, Usable Chat, or an authorised MCP or API integration. Missing, ambiguous, stale, or contradictory answers become handover work. A slide deck that nobody can use after the meeting does not pass.

Connect incidents to relationship memory without copying raw telemetry

Our DevOps investigation pattern moves systematically from reported impact through reproduction, health, traces, logs, events, dependencies, metrics, historical patterns, root-cause hypotheses, approved remediation, and verification. Dead ends are retained because they prevent the next investigator from repeating work. After closure, the useful output becomes two connected records: a detailed investigation and an actionable solution.

For the client relationship, promote only the context that matters: client impact, affected outcome or obligation, confirmed cause, verified restoration, communication decisions, follow-up owner, prevention work, and any changed support or operating guidance. Keep sensitive logs, credentials, internal topology, and live telemetry in their controlled systems.

An incident should improve more than incident response. Its verified lesson can update a runbook, an acceptance test, an onboarding guide, a development standard, a risk review, a future scope assumption, or a carefully reviewed public claim. That is how one event becomes safer delivery across the organisation.

The same connected memory can improve several workflows without collapsing their boundaries:

The gate matters. A client-specific lesson stays inside its private boundary. A reviewer extracts a general method, removes identifying and restricted detail, checks that the claim is supported, assigns an owner and status, and publishes it into the appropriate internal practice or approved public workspace. Linking or symlinking can reduce duplication where permissions genuinely align; it should not be used as a shortcut around client boundaries.

This mirrors our own cross-functional pattern: the consultancy practice records relationship and delivery context, engineering preserves implementation and incident learning, and marketing works from inspectable proof rather than inventing claims. Each area owns its records; Usable makes approved context discoverable to the people and agents who need it.

Govern the memory as carefully as the work

  • Boundary before convenience: isolate client material where confidentiality, contract, personal data, or environment detail requires it. Do not create one searchable pool of every relationship.
  • Least privilege: use workspace roles and narrowly scoped tokens. A receiving client viewer may need read access; an importer may need create access; neither automatically needs administrative control.
  • Minimal data: preserve stakeholder roles and necessary contact context, not indiscriminate personal data. Never store secrets, credentials, raw customer datasets, or unredacted private logs as general memory.
  • Provenance and status: identify the source, author or owner, date, state, approval, and review condition. Keep drafts and hypotheses visibly different from published guidance and accepted decisions.
  • Human authority: assistants may retrieve, draft, compare, and flag conflicts. Humans remain responsible for contractual interpretation, confidentiality, data classification, approval, change authority, and publication.
  • Lifecycle maintenance: archive superseded fragments, preserve relevant decision history, assign review dates, revoke retired integrations, and test whether current answers cite current sources.

Boundary test: if a record names a client, person, private system, contract term, price, environment, incident detail, or unpublished result, keep it in the restricted relationship boundary until an authorised reviewer deliberately produces a safe derivative.

Example: a request moves from promise to continuing service

Imagine an organisation asks a consultancy to add an AI-assisted workflow to an internal process. The CRM remains authoritative for the opportunity. The executed agreement remains in the contract repository. Delivery work stays in the project tracker and repositories. Time and invoices stay in their commercial systems.

In Usable, discovery records why the workflow matters, which data may be used, what “good” means, and which assumptions remain untested. The agreement context links to the signed source and maps outcomes, exclusions, responsibilities, acceptance, and data-handling obligations. A later meeting raises a different authentication approach. The interaction records the request; a decision compares options and consequences; the approved change source confirms whether scope moved.

During delivery, tests, demonstrations, release evidence, training, known limitations, and operational readiness connect back to those outcomes. The receiving team tests the handover by asking how access is controlled, which failure mode is expected, who approves a material change, and where the live sources are. Months later, an incident reveals that one dependency fails differently than assumed. The investigation produces verified cause and recovery guidance; the relationship record links the client impact and follow-up; the runbook and acceptance checks are revised.

A sanitised version of that lesson can strengthen an internal engineering standard and onboarding guide. If it supports an outward claim, Marketing receives a reviewed, inspectable proof artifact rather than private client material. One relationship improves delivery, support, development and communication without leaking across the boundary.

Start with a thin, enforced operating loop

Do not begin by migrating every email and document. Choose one active relationship and enforce a small set of records at the moments where knowledge changes:

  1. Baseline: relationship owner, source-system map, current agreement reference, scope, assumptions, obligations and review date.
  2. After material interactions: decisions, actions, unresolved questions and source evidence captured by an assigned owner.
  3. At milestones and changes: outcome, acceptance evidence, risks, approved scope impact and operating consequence reviewed.
  4. Before handover: receiving team completes a role-based retrieval test and the delivery team resolves gaps.
  5. During service: recurring questions and incidents update current guidance and create reviewed reusable learning.
  6. At renewal or closure: obligations, evidence, open risk, access, retention and successor ownership are reconciled against authoritative records.

Once that loop works, connect approved imports or automations through REST, MCP, webhooks, or other integrations. Automate capture and reminders before automating judgement. Every generated record still needs a clear source, boundary, status, and owner.

What Usable does not decide for you

Usable provides workspaces, focused fragments, types, collections, files, search, human and AI access, and integration surfaces. It can connect the durable context around a relationship. It does not make a draft legally binding, determine whether a contract interpretation is correct, grant permission to reuse client information, replace professional judgement, or guarantee that imported material is accurate.

Your organisation must still choose authoritative systems, contractual and regulatory controls, retention rules, data classification, approval roles, and review cadence. Integrations described here are an operating pattern, not a claim that every connector or automated workflow ships preconfigured. The compounding effect only works when ownership, source quality, boundaries, and maintenance are real.
